## Two datasheets

Put two datasheets side by side on a desk. The first is for a monolithic ceramic and its headline strength is 400 MPa, and the second is for a ceramic matrix composite, a CMC, with a headline strength of 250 MPa. Most buyers, and quite a few engineers, would pick the ceramic, because 400 is a lot more than 250 and the rest of the two sheets look much the same. In this primer I want to show that once you look at the scatter behind those two numbers, you can design a part to a higher stress in the CMC, and that the gap gets wider as the part gets bigger.

In 1963 Peter Medawar gave a talk on the BBC called "Is the Scientific Paper a Fraud?", and his answer was that it is, in a way, because the tidy paper written at the end gives a false picture of the untidy thinking that went into the work. He was not accusing scientists of lying, and I am not accusing datasheets of lying either. A datasheet is a compression of information. It takes tens or hundreds of broken coupons and distils them down to one number, and that number is definitely true about the results but often silent about the spread.

The buyer is not being foolish. If every coupon of the ceramic broke at 400 MPa, the ceramic would be the right choice, and there are jobs where it is. The question is how many of the coupons broke near 400 MPa, and what happened to the rest. In my first job in aerospace I was in charge of qualifying new materials, and I learnt quickly that the headline strength got a material into the design meeting but the basis value (or "design allowable") decided whether it stayed there.

I should say that I have a stake in this. I started a company to make CMCs, so take what follows with that in mind, but I would make the same argument about any two materials with these statistics. The numbers in this piece are synthetic. I generated them from a statistical model so that the shapes are realistic and nobody's real product is being judged, and every figure is labelled to say so.

## The weakest link

Watch a glazier cut a pane of glass. He runs a wheel across it once, leaving a scratch you can barely feel with a fingernail, then puts the line over the edge of the bench and presses, and the pane snaps cleanly along the scratch. The rest of the sheet is just as strong as it was a minute before, but the strength of the whole pane is now set by that one small line.

A monolithic ceramic behaves like the pane. Every piece holds pores, inclusions, grain boundaries that did not quite bond and scratches from machining, and when you load it the crack starts at whichever flaw is worst in the stressed region, and runs straight through with nothing to stop it. Alan Griffith worked this out on glass at the Royal Aircraft Establishment in Farnborough around 1920, and showed that the strength of a brittle solid is set by the size of its biggest crack rather than by the strength of its bonds. That makes ceramic strength a lottery. Each coupon you break is a fresh draw of flaws, and the results spread out with a long tail on the weak side.

The ceramic coupons broke anywhere between 250 and 528 MPa, so the strongest was more than twice as strong as the weakest. The CMC coupons all broke between 215 and 264 MPa. One detail is worth pausing on, because it explains why the problem is so easy to miss. The weakest of the thirty ceramic coupons, at 250 MPa, was still stronger than the average CMC coupon, at 248 MPa. You could test a whole batch of the ceramic and never see a result that worried you, and the statistics would still tell you that the next batch, or the next part, will hold a flaw worse than any you have seen.

## The Weibull modulus

In 1939 a Swedish engineer, Waloddi Weibull, wrote down the statistics of the weakest link. If a material fails from its worst flaw, then the chance that a piece survives a given stress is the chance that every small volume inside it survives, and that leads to a distribution with two numbers. One is a scale, close to the typical strength. The other is the Weibull modulus, m, which describes how tightly the results bunch together.

The easiest way to see m is to plot it. Rank the coupons from weakest to strongest, give each a probability of failure from its rank, and plot those probabilities against strength on axes stretched so that a Weibull distribution falls on a straight line. The slope of that line is m.

<figure>
  <img src="/assets/fig2.jpg" alt="Figure 2">
  <figcaption>Figure 2. Weibull probability plot of the same 60 coupons, with fitted lines. The slope of each line is the Weibull modulus, m. Synthetic data, for illustration only. </figcaption>
</figure>

The ceramic has m = 7.5, a shallow line, which means the flaws vary a lot from one piece to the next and so does the strength. The CMC has m = 29, so steep that almost every coupon fails within a few per cent of 250 MPa. The coefficient of variation tells the same story in more familiar terms, 15.5% for the ceramic and 4.9% for the CMC, and for a Weibull material the two are linked, with the CoV coming out at roughly 1.2 divided by m.

Most datasheets give you the mean, some give you a standard deviation, and very few give you m, which is a shame, because m is the number that tells you how far to trust all the other numbers on the sheet.

## From the mean to A-basis

A designer cannot use the mean. Half the material is weaker than its mean, and nobody wants to fly a part with a one in two chance of being weaker than the number on the drawing. What aerospace uses instead is a basis value. The A-basis is a strength that 99% of the material should exceed, stated with 95% confidence, and it is used where the failure of one part would bring down the structure. The B-basis is the same idea at 90%, used where the load has somewhere else to go. The confidence part matters because thirty coupons is a small sample, and the A-basis has to allow for the chance that the thirty you happened to test were better than the material really is.

Walk both materials down those steps and the ranking changes.

<figure>
  <img src="/assets/fig3.jpg" alt="Figure 3">
  <figcaption>Figure 3. Mean, fitted 1st percentile and A-basis for both materials. A-basis here comes from a parametric bootstrap, not a CMH-17 calculation. Synthetic data, for illustration only.</figcaption>
</figure>

The ceramic starts at 402 MPa, falls to 232 MPa at the fitted 1st percentile, and ends at 198 MPa once the confidence allowance is taken off. The CMC starts at 248 MPa, falls to 216 MPa, and ends at 208 MPa. So the ceramic keeps 49% of its headline number on the way to the design value, the CMC keeps 84%, and the material that looked 60% stronger on the datasheet is now 5% weaker on the drawing.

There is one more detail inside that A-basis number which is easy to miss. With thirty coupons you cannot find the 1st percentile from the data alone, because you have not broken enough coupons to see one. If you refuse to assume any distribution at all, you need 299 specimens to get an A-basis, and so every A-basis from a smaller test has a model choice built into it, usually a Weibull or a normal distribution chosen by testing which one fits. For the numbers here I fitted a Weibull to each set of thirty and used a bootstrap, resampling from the fit thousands of times, to put a confidence bound on the 1st percentile. That is enough to show the shape of the argument, but the formal methods for real allowables, which pool several batches and test the fit properly, are set out in CMH-17, the Composite Materials Handbook, and that is where anyone doing this for a real part should go.

## The size effect

The second penalty arrives after the material has been chosen. A flexure coupon is a small bar, and in a bend test only a thin strip along its lower face carries the highest stress, a few cubic millimetres of material. A real component puts far more material under high stress, and more material means more chances of holding a large flaw, the same way a longer chain is more likely to contain a bad link. For a weakest-link material the strength falls with stressed volume according to a simple rule.

```latex
\sigma_2 = \sigma_1 \left( \frac{V_1}{V_2} \right)^{1/m}
```

The Weibull modulus is back, in the exponent, and so a low-m material pays heavily for being made bigger while a high-m material hardly notices.

</figure>


At a hundred times the stressed volume of the coupon, the ceramic's A-basis falls from 198 MPa to 107 MPa, a loss of 46%, while the CMC's falls from 208 MPa to 177 MPa, a loss of 15%. I have used the same scaling rule for the CMC to keep the comparison fair, but a CMC is not really a single chain of links, and its size dependence comes from other mechanisms and is usually weaker than this.

The same physics explains something that catches people out with ceramics, which is that a bend test gives a higher number than a tensile test on the same material, because the bend test stresses so much less of it. For a high-m material the two numbers sit close together, and for a low-m material they can be far apart, which is one reason the next question to ask of any datasheet is which test produced it.

## Why a CMC can be lifed

Hang a weight from a glass rod and from a rope. The rod holds until the crack at its worst scratch lets go, and then it is in two pieces with no warning. The rope starts to lose strands, one after another, you can hear and see it happening, and it carries on holding the weight long after the first strand has gone.

A CMC is built to behave like the rope. Ceramic fibres sit in a ceramic matrix, and between them is a thin interphase, often carbon or boron nitride, that is deliberately weak. When a crack runs through the matrix and reaches a fibre, the interphase lets it turn and run along the fibre instead of through it, and so the fibres are left intact, bridging the crack and sharing the load. The matrix cracks early and often, but the composite carries on, and its strength comes from a large population of fibres pulling together, which averages out the flaws in any one of them. That is where the high Weibull modulus comes from. (If you want more on how these materials are made, I have written a primer on what a CMC is.)

The statistics are only half the benefit. Because the damage builds up gradually, it can be measured as it happens, from the stiffness dropping, from acoustic emission as cracks form and from inspection, and a failure you can watch coming is one you can model and put a life on. A monolithic ceramic gives you very little to watch.

Monolithic ceramics are not a failure, and they fly and run every day, in bearings and seals and electronics, made safe by proof testing every part and by probabilistic design methods built for exactly this problem, such as NASA's CARES code. They are a different route to the same goal. I should also be honest about the limits of CMCs. The low scatter here is for properties the fibres control, and the properties the matrix controls, like interlaminar shear, scatter much more. At high temperature the real lifing problem is oxygen getting down the matrix cracks to the interphase and the fibres, and that is where most of the hard work in CMCs is being done.

## Three questions

None of this means you should distrust every datasheet you are handed. It means a single strength figure is the start of a conversation, and there are three questions that will tell you most of what the number is leaving out.

The first is what layup was tested. For a composite, the strength depends on the fibre architecture, the fibre volume fraction, the porosity and the process route, and a value measured along the fibres of a 0/90 laminate says very little about a part loaded at 45 degrees or through its thickness.

The second is which standard was used. Flexure gives a different answer from tension, a small coupon gives a different answer from a large one, and room temperature in air gives a different answer from 1,200 °C in steam. For monolithic advanced ceramics the flexure test is ASTM C1161, and the reporting of Weibull parameters is covered by ASTM C1239. For CMCs the flexure test is ASTM C1341 and the tensile test is ASTM C1275. If the datasheet does not name a standard and a temperature, you cannot compare its number with anything.

The third is what scatter came with the number: how many coupons were broken, from how many batches, what the CoV or the Weibull modulus was, and whether a basis value has been worked out. Best of all is to ask for the individual results, because thirty numbers in a spreadsheet tell you more than any summary of them, and a supplier who is confident in their material will usually send them.

If the answers come back thin, it is worth remembering what a headline strength really is. A datasheet is not a description of a material. It is a description of a good day.

## What is at stake

When materials are chosen on headline numbers, the real allowables turn up late, usually after the design has been sized and the tooling ordered, and the cost of finding out lands on the programme at the worst possible moment. There is a quieter cost as well. New materials are compared against incumbents that have decades of test data behind them, and if the comparison is made on means, a newcomer with a tight distribution looks weaker than it is and an incumbent with a wide one looks stronger. Asking for the layup, the standard and the scatter is how a buyer avoids an expensive surprise, and it is also how a new material gets a fair hearing against the ones we already know.
