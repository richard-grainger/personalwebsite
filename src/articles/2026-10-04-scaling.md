Not long ago I made a small batch of ceramic slurry that did exactly what it was supposed to. It mixed smoothly, it behaved the way the method said it would, and it set when it should and not before. I was pleased with it for about as long as it took to try making it again, bigger.

The bigger batch seized in the mixer. Another set before I had finished mixing it. A third refused to come together at all, because one of the powders, bought under exactly the same name as the one in the method I was following, turned out to be a rather different material. None of these was a mistake in the chemistry. The chemistry was the one part of the work I already understood.

I am not saying this as a confession but because it is the most ordinary story in deeptech, and one of the least told. The small sample that works is the thing every new materials company is founded on. It is also a deceptively small step in the journey. It proves that the thing can exist; it says almost nothing about whether you can make it again, in quantity, every time.
That gap is very much at the heart of the deeptech investment memo: the pilot line, the stretch between the paper version and the fully-fledged product. It is often described as a problem of money. In ceramics it is first a problem of physics.

## Three problems in one word

"Scale" sounds like one problem: making more. It is at least three, and they differ in kind.
The first is size: making more, whether that means a bigger batch, a bigger vessel or a bigger part. The second is consistency: making it identically every time, from raw materials, operators and weather that are never quite identical. The third is speed: making it at a rate someone will pay for.

These problems are independent. Solve one and the other two are untouched; often the fix for one aggravates another. Most scale-up failures come from solving one of them and assuming the others would follow.

Ceramics are pretty hostile to all three, for reasons I will talk about, because none of them is to do with luck.

## Bigger: the mixer that seized

The slurry that seized had mixed perfectly in a small vessel. In a wider one, with the same blade, it stalled. The reason is geometry. A mixer does not mix a volume; it mixes the region near its blade, and relies on flow to carry everything else through that region. In a small vessel, everything is near the blade. In a larger one there are margins where the material sits still, and a thick slurry that sits still thickens further. The dead zone grows, the blade works against an increasingly solid wall, and eventually it stops.

The fix was a different kind of mixer, not a more powerful one. That is the general lesson, and it recurs throughout the subject: when a process fails on scaling, the remedy is rarely more of the same. Size changes which mechanism dominates, and the process has to be redesigned around the new one.

The material obeys the same rule. A ceramic fails from its worst flaw, and a larger piece contains more flaws to choose from. This is the Weibull size effect: the same material, made the same way, is statistically weaker in a bigger part. For a typical engineering ceramic, doubling the stressed volume costs several per cent of strength before anything has gone wrong in the making. A coupon tested in the laboratory is not a small sample of the part. It is a different, slightly stronger object.

## Consistently: the material that wasn't

The powder that refused to wet out was ordered by its name in the catalogue, and the name was correct. What the name did not specify was its surface area, and that turned out to be the property that mattered. Ours had many times the surface of the material in the published method, and drank solvent accordingly. The method had not failed. It had described its own raw material accurately; the description simply did not transfer.

A website might describe a material; it does not guarantee one. Commodity raw materials vary in a lot of properties that commodity buyers do not care about, and a new process rarely knows in advance which of those properties it depends on. Much of the work of scale-up is discovering, one surprise at a time, which unlisted variables your process is quietly sensitive to.
Ceramics make that discovery expensive, because their key steps cannot be undone. A slurry that has set cannot be reclaimed. A fired part cannot be reworked. Variation introduced at the start is found at the end, after every hour of labour and furnace time has been spent on it. In a metal, a mistake can often be machined out or heat-treated away. In a ceramic, the mistake is the part.

## Faster: the batch that cured before I was finished

The batch that set in the mixer did so because mixing it took longer than the chemistry allowed. Reactive slurries have a pot life, a window between combining the ingredients and the onset of setting, and that window is fixed by the chemistry. Mixing time is not; it grows with the batch. At the bench the two sit comfortably apart. At a larger scale they meet, and the batch sets before it is ready to use.
The same arithmetic governs the slower steps downstream. Drying, binder burnout and infiltration all depend on something moving through the material, water out or gas in, and diffusion takes time in proportion to the square of the distance travelled. Double the thickness of a part and you roughly quadruple the time it needs. A step that takes an afternoon on a test coupon can take most of a week on a structural component, and the furnace is occupied for all of it.
This is where speed and size collide. The cheapest part to make quickly is a thin one; the part the customer needs is rarely thin.

## What changes when you make a hundred

None of this makes the small batch a mistake. It is where every material has to start, and it answers the first question honestly: can this exist? The difficulty is that its answer is easily mistaken for the answer to the next question.
The more useful question about any new material is not "does it work?" but "what changes when you make a hundred?" Bigger exposes mechanisms the bench never had to face. The same exposes variables nobody wrote down. Faster exposes clocks that were always running but were never short enough to matter.
Answering that question is unglamorous work. It happens in mixers, drying ovens and incoming-goods checks, and it produces knowledge that rarely reaches a paper, because most of it consists of things that should not have mattered and did. It is also, I have come to think, the real substance of a materials company. Anyone can make one good batch. The business is in making the second one boring. I will be writing about that work, process by process, in a series of shorter notes.
A lab result is a proof of existence. A part is a proof of control.
