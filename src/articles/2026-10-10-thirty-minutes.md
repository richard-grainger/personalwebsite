*What happens to a materials engineer's approach when the component is designed to be lost.*

## The ground shifts

This year there has been a political shift that will make a real difference to how systems are designed, and to the balance of properties their materials need. Research and development of new materials is mostly focused on raw performance: strength, toughness, temperature capability, and the manufacturability that turns an interesting coupon into a usable part. But all of these are in tension with cost. Every engineer knows the trade, and most materials roadmaps resolve it the same way: performance first, then bring the cost down, and cross your fingers that the economies of scale work for you.

In aerospace that ordering has rarely been questioned, because the economics allowed it. A part that flies for ten thousand hours can carry a high price, because the price is divided by the hours. The science underneath reflects the same assumption. It is the science of what happens to a material over a long life, and the cost of proving that life is paid once and spread across a fleet.

What has changed is the denominator. The lesson defence planners have drawn from four years of war in Ukraine is that a large share of what is fielded is used once. A cheap drone can be stopped by an interceptor costing many times more, and a defender who answers every drone that way runs out of interceptors, or money, before the attacker runs out of drones. The sector's name for the response is affordable mass: systems cheap enough, and made in enough volume, that cost rather than peak performance sets the limit. When a thing is used once, its price is divided by one. Cost per part stops being a constraint on the specification and becomes part of it.

Britain wrote this into policy in a single sentence. On 2 June 2025 the Strategic Defence Review described what the British Army should look like by 2035, and said it is likely to need a 20-40-40 mix: 20% crewed platforms controlling 40% reusable platforms, meaning drones that survive repeated missions, and 40% 'consumables', a category the review fills with rockets, shells, missiles and one-way effector drones, the whole to be supported by what it calls always-on manufacturing.

Procurement documents rarely contain a word that changes what a material is for. This one does. Between February and June 2026 the word acquired a budget.

On 20 February in Kraków, the five largest European defence spenders launched LEAP, Low-Cost Effectors and Autonomous Platforms, with the first project, a lightweight and affordable surface-to-air weapon, due in 2027, and proposals invited from small firms as well as primes. By July the first contracts under it had been placed: £3.16 million across three British firms to build and trial rival interceptor designs, with demonstrations due before the end of the year. On 27 February the Royal Navy published a request for information for Project METIS, seeking low-cost effectors to sit alongside its complex missiles rather than replace them. On 2 March the Ministry of Defence signed a direct award of just under £5 million for three common one-way-effector launchers, the notice recording a year of mobility, safety and launch trials with the DART250 and the need to equip the Field Army's first one-way-effector detachments. On 10 April the Defence Secretary announced an order for Skyhammer, a jet-powered interceptor launched from a tube at 700 km/h with its own terminal radar, from a company that took the design from drawing board to first flight in six weeks; first deliveries were promised for May and the whole order within six months.

The United States wrote its version down as a specification. The Navy's MACE programme, Multi-mission Affordable Capacity Effector, requires an all-up round costing no more than $300,000 and production of at least 500 a year, and in 2026 it selected a hypersonic missile as the winner. The first budget request covers 353 rounds in fiscal 2027, on the way to roughly 4,500 over five years.

Then on 30 June the Defence Investment Plan was published. It sets out £298 billion over four years, of which more than £5 billion goes to drones and autonomous systems, and £11 billion to munitions and weapons, including low-cost cruise missiles and one-way effectors, with at least six new energetics factories to be built by 2030. It funds an Uncrewed Systems Centre at Swindon and a taskforce whose stated job is to "continuously scale production". It was published nineteen days after the Defence Secretary resigned, warning that the funding behind it fell short. He has been Chancellor of the Exchequer since 20 July, under a new Prime Minister, and the last £4.7 billion of the plan's uplift is to be confirmed at the Budget later this month. The man who resigned over the plan now signs the cheque for it.

I set these out at length because the argument that follows depends on their being real. This is not a think-tank's idea of the future. It is a tender, a direct award, an order, a plan and a Budget.

Which brings me back to the balance of properties. If cost per part is now part of the specification, and the life of the part is measured in minutes, what does the word consumable do to a materials brief?

## Three words

The sector uses three words loosely, and the whole of this piece follows from keeping them apart.

A reusable platform survives repeated missions. That is the old brief; it is what an aircraft has always been, and the whole apparatus of aerospace materials, from alloy specifications to fatigue spectra to inspection intervals, exists to make survival repeatable. An attritable platform may be lost, and is designed so that losing it is acceptable: cheap enough, numerous enough, uncrewed. A consumable will be lost. Losing it is the mission. The Army has since described the same division as survivable, attritable and consumable, and its Chief of the General Staff has said he wants half the money spent on the 20% that is crewed and half on the 80% that is not.

The metric moves with the word. A reusable platform is judged by cost per flight hour, because the hours are what you are buying. A consumable is judged by cost per effect, because the effect is the only thing it ever delivers. Life stops being a design target and becomes a mission duration. A jet-powered one-way effector of the class the Army has been trialling weighs about 85 kg and covers something over 250 kilometres at something over 400 km/h, which is to say that its working life is a little over half an hour. A material's job in it is to survive the mission and nothing after.

## The rent inversion

In the primer on oxidation I argued that every long-life hot structure pays rent to time. The rent is collected slowly: oxidation, creep, fatigue, moisture, and the interaction of all four. I called the intermediate-temperature range the danger zone because it is where the payments collect, where a silicon carbide surface stops protecting itself and a carbon fibre begins to burn from within along its own interphase. The discipline of environmental barrier coatings exists to defer that rent. The discipline of lifing exists to predict when it falls due.

A consumable never reaches the danger zone. Its time axis is thirty minutes long. Oxidation that would destroy a component in a thousand hours has not begun after thirty minutes; creep that would distort it in ten thousand does not register; a fatigue crack that would grow to critical length over a service life has one cycle in which to do so. The walls that nature builds slowly never arrive.

Consider what that admits to the menu. Carbon fibre in a hot structure with no coating at all, because the fibre will not have time to oxidise before the mission ends. A matrix chosen for how quickly it can be processed rather than for how long it can survive. No environmental barrier coating, because there is no environment to bar for long enough to matter. Materials that fail at ten thousand hours and are indifferent at thirty minutes are exactly the materials a long-life brief excludes, and exactly the materials a consumable brief can use.

That is half the argument, and it is the half that reads as good news for anyone who makes hot, light, cheap things. The other half is that the time wall does not vanish. It moves.

A consumable spends years in a magazine before its thirty minutes. The service life is short; the shelf life is long, and it is spent in poor conditions for a material: sealed in a container, through summers and winters, humidity and thermal cycling, transport by sea and road. Shelf life replaces service life. Storage humidity and thermal cycling replace flight hours. A matrix with water in its chemistry, as inorganic matrices have, must answer for what that water does over a decade in a box, and the answer is as real as any creep curve. I will keep those questions for a more technical setting; the point here is that they exist. The right brief for a consumable is not "design for thirty minutes". It is: design for the life the thing actually has, which is years asleep and minutes awake.

## The rate inversion

In the hypersonics piece I argued that the field is gated not by design but by cost per part at rate: the thousandth part with the line running, not the first part on a bench. Affordable mass takes that observation and writes it into the specification.

MACE does so in numbers, $300,000 and 500 a year, and a hypersonic weapon has been selected against them. The first budget line prices the initial lot above the ceiling, on the expectation that volume will bring it down, and the company chosen has put $220 million of its own money into a 1,000-acre plant in New Mexico to make sure that it does. The SDR does so in a phrase, always-on manufacturing. The Defence Investment Plan does so in bricks: six energetics factories and a taskforce charged with scaling production. Skyhammer does so in a calendar. Development began in January 2025 and the first flight came within six weeks; the order came fourteen months later and deliveries the month after that.

The consequence for materials is exact. A process route with weeks in a furnace is excluded by definition, whatever the coupon data say. The route that can meet 500 a year, or 5,000, has polymer-class cycle times and runs on the composites base that already exists: presses, autoclaves, tape lines, the kit that makes wind blades and car parts. The exquisite is not wrong. It is disqualified from this category by the calendar.

That disqualification is also what makes a British capability plausible again. In *Britain's High-Temperature Materials Gap* I argued that the gap is industrial rather than intellectual: the country knows how to make these materials and has no volume base for making them. A volume base needs a customer who wants volume. For once, the customer is asking for it in writing.

There is a photograph from the Office of War Information of a Liberty ship sliding down the ways at Bethlehem-Fairfield in Baltimore in the spring of 1943, and its original caption records that the keel plates were laid twenty-four days earlier. The Liberty ship was designed for a five-year life, welded rather than riveted so that it could be built by people who had never built a ship, and launched faster than the enemy could sink it. Always-on manufacturing is a phrase from 2025. Built to be lost is a design principle from 1941.

## Wood and mild steel

Affordable mass at high performance has a British precedent, and the one-way effector has an ancestor. Both come from the same war.

The de Havilland Mosquito was built of balsa and birch plywood because aluminium was strategic and the aircraft factories were full. The fuselage was a sandwich of balsa between birch skins, moulded in two half-shells and glued, and the work was spread across hundreds of furniture makers, piano manufacturers and coachbuilders who had never built an aircraft before. There is a photograph in the Imperial War Museum of Mosquito tailplanes being assembled at Hoopers, the coachbuilders, at Park Royal. It is the argument of the previous section in a single image: a manufacturing base borrowed from another industry, because the one you would have chosen is busy.

What was not surrendered was performance. The prototype flew in November 1940 and was faster than the Spitfire that was meant to escort it; 7,781 were built. Availability chose the material and the factory. The material and the factory did not choose the speed.

The V-1 was mild steel and plywood around a pulsejet, launched from ramps in northern France from 13 June 1944. It was built to be lost, and it was lost by the thousand. Some ten thousand were fired at Britain; 2,419 reached the London area; 6,184 civilians were killed and 17,981 injured. Thousands more died under the ones aimed at Antwerp and Liège. It was the first one-way effector, and the line from the damaged ramp at Belloy-sur-Somme to the launchers bought this March is straight. I include it because the lineage is real and cannot be left out, not because it is a curiosity.

## The qualification inversion

Qualification changes shape too, and this is the inversion that decides how fast a new material can enter service.

Qualifying a material for a reusable airframe is a lifing exercise. The questions are how many cycles before inspection, how many before retirement, what the damage looks like as it grows and how reliably it can be found. Those questions take years to answer because they are questions about years, and no cleverness in the test rig shortens the calendar the test is meant to simulate.

Qualifying a material for a consumable is a lot-acceptance exercise. The question is whether this batch does what the last batch did. It is a question about consistency, not endurance, and with the right instruments and a disciplined process it can be answered in weeks. A different question, on a different timescale, at a different cost.

The inversion is in lifing, not in safety. Storage, handling and launch safety are as strict for a consumable as they have ever been, and rightly: the thing sits in a magazine next to other things and next to people. What is relaxed is the requirement to prove a long life, because there is no long life to prove.

This is where a new material can enter service in years rather than decades, provided it can be shown to be the same every time. That proviso is the whole of the work. The consumable brief takes down the wall of time and puts up a wall of repeatability in its place, which is a wall a manufacturer can see and climb. The definitions, qualification against characterisation against certification, are in the companion primer published alongside this piece, and I will not repeat them here.

## The corner entered by demand

In *Hot, Light, Cheap* I drew the map as a triangle, and the corner where all three meet as the place invention had not yet reached. The assumption behind every materials roadmap I have read is that invention enters that corner first and demand follows. The documents above describe the reverse. Demand is entering the corner first. A consumable brief asks for hot, light and cheap at once, and forgives the one thing, life, that made the corner hard to reach. I should declare an interest, as I did in the piece on Britain's gap: I am a founder of a company that makes composites for precisely this temperature range, and I have a stake in the argument. The argument does not rest on the stake. It rests on a design principle that the procurement documents have reached before the materials community has: design for the life the mission actually has, not the life the certification culture assumes.
