*Three words people use interchangeably, and shouldn't.*

If you buy a loaf from a local baker, you can learn a lot about it on the walk home. You can weigh it, squeeze it and look at the crumb, and if you were so inclined you could send a slice to a lab and find out exactly how much salt and water and air is in it. That tells you what that loaf is. What it can't tell you is whether tomorrow's loaf will be the same, and if you were a supermarket buying ten thousand loaves a week, that second question is the only one you would really care about. And before the supermarket sells a single one, somebody from the council will have looked round the kitchen and agreed that it is fit to feed the public.

Those are three different questions, asked by three different people, and in materials engineering they have three different names: characterisation, qualification and certification. They get swapped around freely in meetings and funding calls and press releases (and I have probably done it myself in my early career), but they mean quite different things, and the difference between them is where most new materials spend their time.

## Characterisation

Characterisation is finding out what a material is and how it behaves. It is the part most people picture when they think of materials science: tensile tests and thermal analysis and microscopes and dielectric measurements, and the long tables of numbers that end up on a datasheet. It answers how strong, how stiff, how hot, how heavy, and how it breaks.

The thing to hold on to is that characterisation describes the samples you tested, and only those samples. A university lab can characterise a brilliant new composite from a dozen coupons made by one careful PhD student on a good afternoon, and every number in the paper will be true. It just isn't yet a promise about the next batch, or the one made by somebody else, in a different building, in February.

## Qualification

Qualification is proving that the material is the same every time. It takes the characterisation numbers and asks whether they survive contact with different batches of raw material, different operators, different days, different humidity, and the slow drift of every machine on the line. For a structural aerospace material that means testing several batches, all made to a fixed process specification, enough times that the statistics can tell you the strength you can actually rely on rather than the average. That design value is always lower than the headline number, sometimes a lot lower, and I went through why in Show Me the Scatter.

The specification is the heart of it. Once a material is qualified the recipe and the process are fixed, and changing the supplier of one raw material, or the temperature of one oven, can mean repeating a good part of the work. So qualification is as much a claim about the factory as it is about the chemistry. Repeatability is a measurement claim before it is a materials claim, and you cannot qualify what you cannot measure on the line.

## Certification

Certification is someone official agreeing. That might be the Civil Aviation Authority, EASA or the FAA for a civil aircraft, or the Military Aviation Authority and the programme's own airworthiness process for a defence one. They look at the evidence and accept that a part, an engine or a whole aircraft is safe to fly.

The part that surprises people outside the industry is that materials are not normally certified on their own. Parts and engines and aircraft are certified, and the qualified material goes along inside them as part of the evidence. So a material can be fully qualified and still never fly, because nobody has yet put it into a design that someone official has signed off.

## Why the order matters

Each step stands on the one before it. You can't qualify something you haven't characterised, and you can't certify a part made from something that isn't qualified, and the effort grows at every step. Engineers draw this as a pyramid, with thousands of small coupon tests at the bottom, then elements and joints, then sub-components and components, and a handful of full-scale tests at the top. The material lives at the bottom of that pyramid, which means its clock starts first and stops last.

British carbon fibre is the classic lesson in what happens when the steps are rushed. The fibre was invented at Farnborough in the mid-1960s and within a few years was being made into fan blades for the RB211, and when those blades struggled with bird strike in testing the programme fell back on titanium. Much of what a modern qualification asks for, hot and wet conditioning, impact damage, batch to batch scatter, reads like a list of things that generation didn't yet know they needed to test. A qualification checklist is mostly a record of things somebody once found out the hard way, written down so that nobody has to find them out again.

## What it means for a new material

I started my career in aerospace in charge of qualifying new materials, and I now sit on the other side of the table trying to get one through. From both sides, the most useful thing I can offer anyone hearing about a new material is to ask which of the three words actually applies. A startup can characterise a new chemistry in months, it will take far longer to qualify it, and certification only happens when a customer puts it into a part and takes it to a regulator.

None of that is a reason not to try. But when the three words get blurred, investors expect a product where there is still a programme of work, funders judge the wrong milestone, and good materials get written off as failures for not doing in two years what was always going to take longer. Being precise about which question has been answered is the cheapest way I know to keep a new material alive long enough to answer the next one.
